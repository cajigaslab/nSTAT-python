Continuous-time CIF simulator — filtered stimulus drive + inhibitory self-history

Question: Does a continuous LTI stimulus filter integrated with scipy.signal.lsim reproduce closed-form linear-systems-theory filtering, and does a nonzero continuous self-history feedback filter measurably regularize spiking relative to no history?

Goal: Confirm the continuous LTI realization matches known filter theory (the numerically realised ``scipy.signal.lsim`` drive agrees with the closed-form steady-state sinusoidal response once the initial transient dies out), and confirm the nonzero continuous self-history filter produces the expected qualitative refractory signature: a lower interspike-interval coefficient of variation relative to the same drive with no self-history feedback.

Native-Python port of the MATLAB Simulink model ``PointProcessSimulationCont.slx`` (:func:`nstat.extras.continuous_cif.simulate_cif_continuous`). A single simulated cortical unit's firing probability is driven by a slow 1 Hz oscillatory "LFP" stimulus, filtered through a continuous first-order lowpass synaptic filter ``S(s) = K/(tau*s + 1))`` integrated with ``scipy.signal.lsim`` -- continuous-time filtering, not a discrete pre-sampled sequence. Independently, the unit carries an inhibitory continuous self-history filter ``H(s) = -G/(tau_h*s + 1)`` modeling post-spike refractoriness as a continuous decay rather than a fixed-bin dead time. Two runs share the exact same injected ``U(0,1)`` variates -- one with ``H = 0``, one with the nonzero inhibitory ``H`` -- so any difference in the realised spike train is attributable to ``H`` alone.

Clusterless decoding vs. a sorted decoder as spike sorting degrades

Question: Can we decode kinematics/position from unsorted multiunit events + waveform marks, and does clusterless decoding match/beat sorted decoding as sorting quality degrades?

Goal: Answer the motor/hippocampal decoding question directly: does clusterless decoding match or beat sorted decoding as sorting degrades? The clusterless decoder degrades gracefully (it never used unit identity in the first place) while the sorted comparator holds up at low sort-error rates and then collapses once corrupted labels dominate — filling the Tier 2.1 gap in ``parity/methods_roadmap.md`` with a concrete clusterless-vs-sorted robustness story rather than a bare bridge smoke test.

Four place-/kinematic-tuned units ride a synthetic 1-D back-and-forth trajectory and emit **unsorted** multiunit events on a single tetrode: which unit generated a given event is ambiguous, and its 4-channel waveform mark is drawn from overlapping per-unit clusters (Kloosterman 2014; Deng 2015). ``fit_clusterless_decoder`` decodes position directly from the never-sorted marks (Denovellis 2021, eLife) exactly once, since its accuracy cannot depend on spike-sorting quality. A comparator built from the same upstream library's ``SortedSpikesDecoder`` then decodes from spike trains whose true unit-identity labels are corrupted at a tunable sort-error rate — a stand-in for cumulative merge/split spike-sorting mistakes — swept from a perfect sort up to fully scrambled labels. Also runs the discrete-state classifier (``fit_clusterless_classifier``).

Population-vector bias vs. maximum-likelihood decode of reach direction

Question: Is the classic population vector (Georgopoulos et al. 1982/1986) biased when M1 preferred directions are non-uniformly distributed, and does a maximum-likelihood/Bayesian tuning-curve decode (Sanger 1996) stay unbiased where the population vector fails?

Goal: Reproduce Sanger's (1996) central result directly: population-vector decoding implicitly assumes preferred directions uniformly tile the circle, so it is systematically biased toward the dense preferred-direction region when they do not, while the maximum-likelihood/probability-density decoder stays unbiased.

An 80-unit M1 population performs an 8-direction center-out task (Georgopoulos et al. 1982). Preferred directions are deliberately clustered rather than uniform (Sanger 1996's critique), each unit's von-Mises-shaped tuning curve is fit from training trials with ``nstat.fit_poisson_glm``, and held-out trials are decoded with (a) the classic Georgopoulos population vector and (b) a maximum-likelihood grid search over the fitted tuning curves.

Motor-BCI decoder calibration — naive-KF vs. ReFIT-KF recalibration

Question: How should a decoder be calibrated from neural + behavioral training data, and does closed-loop-aware (ReFIT) recalibration correct a systematic directional bias baked into an open-loop-calibrated decoder's training label, relative to a naively calibrated Kalman decoder?

Goal: Show that decoder *calibration*, not just decoder architecture, determines closed-loop BCI performance: the naive decoder's uncorrected directional bias makes it mis-aim on every reach, so it curves away from every target and never acquires it, while ReFIT recalibration -- relabeling the same neural data with the intended, target-directed kinematics -- corrects the bias and acquires all 8 targets, illustrating in miniature the ReFIT-relabeling principle of Gilja et al. (2012) (this demo does not measure target-acquisition time and does not reproduce their specific acquisition-time speedup). Also exercises the opt-in, JAX-backed Dynamax alternatives to the KF_EM / PP_EM / mPPCO_EM families (whose MATLAB mirrors are native in DecodingAlgorithms), plus the honest fit-quality metric (predictive log-likelihood) on which all three EM workflows are compared.

A Wu (2006)-style linear-Gaussian arm state (position/velocity) coupled to a 40-unit population with linear velocity encoding drives an 8-target center-out reach+hold task. A naive-KF decoder is calibrated from a constant-velocity, never-decelerating training label with a directional drift that is never corrected (a stand-in for open-loop/passive calibration blocks that never captured the deceleration/stop epoch of a real reach); a ReFIT-KF decoder (Gilja, Nuyujukian, Chestek, Cunningham, Yu, Fan, Churchland, Kaufman, Kao, Ryu & Shenoy 2012) is recalibrated by relabeling the *same* recorded neural data with the true target-directed, decelerating kinematics. Both share one generic Kalman motion model and are decoded with ``nstat.decoding_algorithms.DecodingAlgorithms.kalman_filter`` on held-out trials -- a pure NumPy/SciPy contrast that always runs, with no optional dependency. An opt-in appendix (behind the ``[dynamax]`` extra) then walks through every routine in ``nstat.extras.em.dynamax_bridge``: linear-Gaussian EM (KF_EM equivalent), CMGF Poisson filter/smoother on a known Poisson-LGSSM (the point-process adaptive-filtering framework of Eden, Frank, Barbieri, Solo & Brown 2004), point-process EM (PP_EM), hybrid Poisson+Gaussian EM (mPPCO_EM), and a pure-NumPy held-out predictive log-likelihood.

Goal: Confirm the modulated-renewal CIF recovers, from spikes alone, the three-way firing-phenotype separation neurosurgeons use to confirm STN/GPi MER targeting (Bergman et al. 1994; Levy et al. 2000; Sterio et al. 2002; Sedov et al. 2025): tonic-regular units show low renewal CV, irregular units show a Poisson-like renewal CV, and tremor-locked bursting units show a Poisson-like renewal CV *but* an elevated, oscillation-driven burst index -- the statistic that separates rate-modulation-driven bursting from plain irregularity.

Clinical velocity Kalman decoder — pykalman cross-check + chronic-drift recalibration

Question: Does the clinical workhorse velocity Kalman decoder (Wu 2006; Malik et al. 2011) replicate across independent implementations, and how does its decode accuracy degrade under chronic per-unit drift (Perge et al. 2013) and unit turnover (Downey et al. 2018) — and is periodic recalibration what restores it?

Goal: Show that the clinical velocity Kalman decoder's numerics replicate across independent implementations (the ``assert_filtered_agree`` regression guard), and that its real vulnerability in a chronic intracortical BCI is not the algorithm but the nonstationary population underneath it: a decoder frozen at Day-0 calibration decodes a steadily worsening velocity correlation coefficient as per-unit drift and unit turnover accumulate, while a decoder periodically recalibrated from a brief same-day calibration block holds its accuracy close to Day-0 levels throughout -- illustrating why periodic recalibration, not decoder architecture alone, is what keeps a chronic BCI clinically usable session after session.
